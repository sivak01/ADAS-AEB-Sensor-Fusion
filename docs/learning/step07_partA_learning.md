# Step 07 Part A — learning note

## The idea in plain language (≤ 8 sentences)
Time-to-collision is conceptually simple: distance divided by how fast the gap is closing. But "how fast the gap is closing" is the part almost every project gets wrong at least once. It is NOT the other vehicle's raw speed (a car doing 30 mph next to you at 30 mph isn't closing on you at all) — it's the *relative* motion between the two of you, projected onto the line connecting you, so it accounts for your own speed too. This function computes exactly that: take the relative velocity, take the direction from you to the object, and see how much of that relative velocity points along that direction. Two safety nets sit around the core formula: one that ignores an object's velocity entirely if it's too small to be trusted (sensor jitter on something actually stationary), and one that treats a *barely* positive closing speed the same as no real closing speed at all, rather than reporting a technically-true but meaningless "you'll collide in 11 hours."

## Tiny worked numeric example
An object sits 30 m directly ahead of the ego vehicle and isn't moving. The ego vehicle is driving directly toward it at 10 m/s. The gap between them shrinks by exactly 10 m every second (since the object stands still, all of the closing is the ego's own approach speed) — so closing speed = 10 m/s, and time-to-collision = 30 m ÷ 10 m/s = **3.0 seconds**. Now suppose that object is actually reported as moving very slightly — 0.2 m/s of sensor jitter, direction irrelevant — well under this project's trusted-speed floor of 0.5 m/s. The function throws that reading away and treats the object as if it reported exactly 0 m/s, so the answer comes out **identical**: 3.0 seconds, not a slightly-different number contaminated by noise that was never real motion in the first place.

## Why it is designed this way (which past mistake it prevents — cite what-not-to-do.md section)
This step is a direct build-out of what-not-to-do.md §5's five sub-rules, all in one function: closing speed uses relative motion, not raw object velocity (preventing the "two cars traveling together look dangerous" false positive); a closing deadband exists separately from `<=0` (preventing a technically-positive-but-negligible closing speed from producing a nonsense multi-hour TTC — v2's real, measured version of this bug reached ~190 million seconds before it was caught); the two deadbands (trusted-speed vs. closing-speed) are kept structurally separate rather than merged, because an object can have plenty of raw speed while still barely closing on the ego vehicle (tangential motion) — conflating the two would silently miss that case. A subtler lesson, not explicit in what-not-to-do.md but very much in its spirit: even a carefully-written specification document can contain an internal sign error, and the fix isn't to trust the prose over the worked example — it's to notice the contradiction, verify it numerically, and resolve it in favor of whichever reading is physically correct and matches the test, documenting the discrepancy rather than silently picking one.

## How to explain this in an interview (3 bullets)
- "Closing speed is relative velocity projected onto the line of sight, not either party's raw speed — I verified this with a rotation/translation-invariance test: applying an arbitrary rigid transform to the whole scene (both positions and velocities) must leave the computed TTC completely unchanged, which is a strong, hard-to-fake check that the geometry is truly relative rather than accidentally anchored to one coordinate axis."
- "Before implementing the spec's formula, I checked it numerically against its own worked example and found a sign inconsistency — the literal prose formula gave −10 m/s where the test explicitly required +10 m/s. I resolved it in favor of the physically correct, test-matching convention and documented the exact discrepancy in the code, rather than silently guessing or blindly implementing the literal text."
- "I kept this function completely pure — no config-file reads, no I/O, no hidden state — specifically so the exact same code path can be used for both the live tracking pipeline and the ground-truth evaluation builder, which is what makes a 'did the system get the right answer' comparison meaningful instead of comparing two subtly different formulas."

## Common misunderstandings
- **"A negative closing speed and a below-threshold closing speed are the same case."** They're related but reported with different reasons on purpose (`OPENING` vs. `CLOSING_DEADBAND`) — both result in TTC = infinity, but "actively moving apart" and "technically approaching but too slowly to matter" are different diagnostic situations worth telling apart later.
- **"If an object's speed is exactly zero, the trusted-speed deadband doesn't apply — that check is only for noisy near-zero readings."** In this implementation it applies uniformly: a literal zero and a jitter reading below the trust threshold are handled by the identical code path, which is what makes "a jittery near-stationary object behaves identically to a truly stationary one" (A4 test 5) not just true in effect but true by construction.
- **"Doubling the distance at the same closing speed doesn't obviously have to double the TTC — that depends on the model."** It's actually a direct consequence of TTC being a simple ratio (distance ÷ closing speed) with no other distance-dependent terms — this is a good sanity check specifically because a bug that introduced some other distance-dependence (e.g., a hidden deceleration model) would break this simple linear relationship.
