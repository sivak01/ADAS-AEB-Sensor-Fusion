# Step 05 Part A — learning note

## The idea in plain language (≤ 8 sentences)
Not every object in a scene matters to a collision-avoidance system — a pedestrian on the sidewalk 20 meters to the side is irrelevant no matter how fast they're walking, but tracking and evaluating TTC for them anyway would waste compute and could generate false alarms. This step draws a simple rectangular "corridor" directly ahead of the vehicle — some width, some maximum range — and only objects inside it get treated as real candidates. But there's a chicken-and-egg problem: at the very first moment an object is detected, there's no velocity yet (that takes at least two sightings), so a strict corridor could reject something that's about to swerve or drift into the danger zone a moment later. The fix used here is a two-stage filter: an early, generous "might matter" gate based on position alone (wider than the real corridor, to avoid missing anything), and — once a track has a real velocity (a later step) — a stricter, motion-aware "is it actually heading into the path" test. This step only builds the first stage: the geometry and the generous gate.

## Tiny worked numeric example
The tight corridor is 1.2 m to either side of the vehicle's centerline (2.4 m total, roughly one car-width beyond typical lane markings) and up to 60 m ahead. A pedestrian standing steady at 1.0 m to the side and 15 m ahead is inside the tight corridor (1.0 ≤ 1.2). A cyclist riding right at the edge, 1.4 m to the side, is just outside the tight corridor — but the *candidate gate* (used before any velocity is known) widens that boundary by another 1.5 m to 2.7 m total, so the cyclist still gets picked up as a candidate worth tracking, even though they wouldn't yet count as "in the path" for TTC purposes.

## Why it is designed this way (which past mistake it prevents — cite what-not-to-do.md section)
This step doesn't correct a specific V1/V2 bug the way step02/03/07A did — the predecessor project never built anything like this at all, because it tracked every object in a 20-second scene as a matter of design (full multi-object tracking), not just the ones that matter for an immediate braking decision. The design principle instead comes straight from `CLAUDE_Instant TTC Fusion.md`'s own design contract: *"Forward-path relevance filter... this project has no reason to track something off to the side that will never matter for a driving decision."* The curved-road limitation is deliberately left unsolved and explicitly documented rather than quietly patched — a straight-line corridor is wrong on an actual curve or at an intersection, and pretending otherwise (or half-solving it with an ad hoc curve-fit) would hide a real, known gap instead of stating it plainly, which is exactly the kind of honesty `what-not-to-do.md` asks for throughout the project.

## How to explain this in an interview (3 bullets)
- "I split the forward-path filter into two deliberately different strictness levels for a specific reason: at detection time there's no velocity yet, so being too strict early would silently drop an object that's about to matter, while being appropriately strict later — once a track has real velocity data — avoids wasting tracking effort on things that will never be relevant."
- "For ground-truth boxes, I implemented the exact geometric rule the project specified — the *nearest point of the object's footprint* to the vehicle, not its center — which meant writing real closest-point-on-a-polygon geometry rather than a shortcut, and I proved it does what it's supposed to with a test where most of an object's footprint sits outside the corridor but its nearest edge doesn't, and confirmed it still counts as relevant."
- "I explicitly did not try to solve curved roads or intersections here — I documented it as a known, stated limitation instead, because half-solving it with an ad hoc curve model would have created a false sense of coverage without any real validation behind it."

## Common misunderstandings
- **"The wider 'candidate gate' and the strict corridor are just different names for the same thing."** They serve different jobs at different times: the candidate gate exists purely because velocity isn't known yet at detection time, and gets replaced by a stricter, motion-aware test (Part B) the moment a track has enough history to support one.
- **"Nearest-point-of-the-footprint and centroid-of-the-footprint would usually give the same answer for corridor membership."** Not for a large or partially-observed object near the corridor's edge — a bus whose center sits well outside the corridor but whose near corner pokes into it is a real, different-answer case, and it's exactly the DEC-1 nearest-surface decision this project made on purpose (consistent with using the same "nearest surface" definition everywhere, including for TTC distance itself).
- **"x=0 in the ego frame (right at the vehicle) should count as 'in the corridor.'"** It's deliberately excluded (`0 < x`, not `0 <= x`) — a point exactly level with the ego isn't ahead of it in any meaningful sense for a forward-looking corridor.
