# Step 04a — learning note

## The idea in plain language (≤ 8 sentences)
A LiDAR sensor doesn't hand you a list of "here's a car, here's a pedestrian" — it hands you a raw cloud of tens of thousands of individual 3D points, most of which just bounced off the road surface. Turning that cloud into something a tracker can use means, first, throwing away the ground (fit a flat plane to the low, nearby points and drop anything close to it), then grouping whatever's left into clumps that are probably one physical object each (clustering), and finally picking one representative point per clump to hand to the tracker. That last choice matters more than it sounds: this project always picks the *nearest point of the clump to the ego vehicle*, not its average position (centroid), because for collision timing the only thing that matters is how far away the closest bit of that object actually is — a car's centroid can be 2 metres further away than its bumper, and 2 metres is the difference between "still safe" and "should have braked already." A LiDAR sensor also has a blind spot literally on top of itself: its own roof mount reflects the beam right back, and without explicitly excluding that near-zero range, the sensor appears to see a solid 10,000-point "wall" that is actually itself.

## Tiny worked numeric example
A parked car's LiDAR points span x = 24m to 28m from the ego vehicle (a 4m-long car, nose-first). The **centroid** reference point sits at x = 26m. The **nearest-surface** reference point (DEC-1) sits at x = 24m — the near bumper, closest point of the cluster. If the ego vehicle is closing at 10 m/s, using the centroid gives TTC = 26/10 = **2.6 seconds**; using nearest-surface gives TTC = 24/10 = **2.4 seconds**. A system tuned to brake at a 2.5s threshold would stay silent with the centroid reference and correctly brake with the nearest-surface one — a 0.2-second, 2-metre bias is exactly the margin an AEB threshold lives or dies on.

## Why it is designed this way (which past mistake it prevents — cite the predecessor project's own lesson)
The predecessor project (V1) tried to measure its own LiDAR/radar sensor noise (R) empirically, by comparing detected positions against ground truth — and found the number it got back (~2.0m) was physically implausible for LiDAR, because its detections were badly fragmented (4.1x more detections than real objects), so only ~86% of the matches it thought were "real" actually were, corrupting the residual statistic below a trustworthy inlier rate (lessons-from-v1-v2.md item A.10). This step repeats that exact experiment on this project's own, much simpler adapter (`measure_sensor_R.py`) — and states an explicit "never silently swap in a measured value" rule as a direct consequence: even getting a clean 81% inlier rate this time (comfortably above the 70% bar) isn't enough on its own, because the sample behind it is thin (16 objects) and the matching is still nearest-neighbour-only, not real tracked-object identity. The lesson carried forward isn't "R can never be measured" — it's "a measured-looking number still needs a human decision before it's trusted," which is why this step reports the result and asks, rather than updating config.py on its own.

## How to explain this in an interview (3 bullets)
- "I built the LiDAR object-detection front end from scratch: ground-plane removal via RANSAC, DBSCAN clustering on the remaining points, and a deliberate choice of the cluster's nearest-to-ego point (not its centroid) as the reference position, because for a collision-timing system the near surface is what physically matters, not the object's average position."
- "On real data, I caught a genuine sensor artifact before it corrupted anything downstream: the LiDAR's own vehicle mount was reflecting the beam back at point-blank range, showing up as a single ~10,000-point 'object' sitting on top of the ego vehicle. I diagnosed it by checking the fitted ground plane was correct first (it was), then isolating the two largest clusters and inspecting their raw coordinate ranges directly — which pointed straight at the sensor's own mount height and near-zero range — before adding a minimum-range exclusion and re-verifying the fix against multiple scenes."
- "I also caught my own process mistake mid-investigation: an evidence script I'd written was pulling real-data examples from a scene that was actually assigned to the held-out evaluation split, not the tuning split I meant to use — a direct violation of this project's own data-discipline rule. I corrected it, deleted the affected figures, and re-ran everything against the correct tuning-split scene before finishing the step, rather than letting a convenient-looking result stand on a technicality."

## Common misunderstandings
- **"More detections than real objects always means a bug."** Not necessarily — this step's adapter deliberately casts a wide net (a coarse region-of-interest, no size/shape filtering) because narrowing that down to "is this actually in my driving path" is a *later* step's job (step05's candidate gate), done with different, cheaper logic. A LiDAR adapter that only ever returns exactly the "interesting" objects would be quietly doing filtering it isn't supposed to do at this stage, and would be much harder to trust as a general-purpose sensor front end.
- **"If a sensor's own scoring variant says '100% correct,' fusion has nothing to add there."** The unrestricted-GT number for the same run said "100% missed" on the exact same danger events — the sensor's 100% success rate is only over the (zero) events it could physically observe. Both numbers are true at once and are answering different questions; the gap between them is precisely the argument for why a single sensor isn't the whole story.
- **"A minimum-range cutoff is just a magic number to make the problem go away."** It's a direct, measured fix for a real, physical phenomenon (a sensor's beam reflecting off its own mounting structure) — the exact value (3.0m) was chosen because it's larger than a passenger vehicle's own body extent from a roof-mounted sensor, not tuned against an outcome metric, which is why it's recorded as ASSUMED (a physically reasoned choice) rather than something that needed the tuning split to arrive at.
