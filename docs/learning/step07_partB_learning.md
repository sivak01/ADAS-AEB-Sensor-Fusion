# Step 07 Part B — learning note

## The idea in plain language (≤ 8 sentences)
Part A built a pure formula: hand it a position, a velocity, and it hands back a time-to-collision. This step connects that formula to real, live tracked objects, which introduces two problems a pure formula alone can't solve. First: not every track deserves to be trusted yet — a track that's only been seen once, or whose velocity estimate is still too shaky, shouldn't be allowed to produce a confident-looking number at all, so this step adds an eligibility gate that explicitly marks those cases as "no usable estimate" rather than quietly guessing. Second: a real vehicle might have several objects in its path at once, but can only act on one decision at a time, so this step also picks the single most urgent one — the path-relevant object with the smallest genuine time-to-collision — while completely ignoring anything outside the driving corridor, no matter how close it happens to be.

## Tiny worked numeric example
Three tracked objects exist at once: one 50 meters ahead and stationary (TTC around 5 seconds at 10 m/s closing), one 15 meters ahead and stationary (TTC around 1.5 seconds), and one only 5 meters away but sitting 20 meters off to the side of the road, outside the driving corridor entirely. A naive "just pick the nearest thing" rule would wrongly choose the 5-meter object — but it isn't in the vehicle's path at all, so it's excluded before distance is ever compared. Between the two genuinely in-path objects, the 15-meter one (TTC ≈ 1.5s) is correctly chosen over the 50-meter one (TTC ≈ 5s), because urgency is measured in time, not raw distance.

## Why it is designed this way (which past mistake it prevents — cite what-not-to-do.md section)
"Do not treat NOT_ELIGIBLE as safe: it must be visible downstream" (step07 §B3) is the central design rule here, and it connects directly to what-not-to-do.md's overall theme of never letting an unknown quietly masquerade as a known-good answer. A system that silently treated "I don't have enough information yet" the same as "nothing dangerous is happening" would be actively dangerous in exactly the moments early in an object's detection history when caution matters most. The strict separation between the point-estimate TTC (what actually drives action, per DEC-10) and the conservative bound (visible, but not acted on yet) is the same honesty discipline applied to uncertainty itself: the system is allowed to know it's uncertain without that uncertainty being hidden, but also without prematurely making every decision maximally cautious before that tradeoff has actually been evaluated.

## How to explain this in an interview (3 bullets)
- "I kept 'is this track trustworthy enough to act on' as a completely separate, reusable check from the TTC math itself — the exact same eligibility function is used both to decide whether an object counts as 'entering' the driving path and whether its TTC estimate is usable at all, so there's only one definition of 'trustworthy velocity' in the whole system, not two that could quietly drift apart."
- "I proved the causality requirement actually matters with a test, not just a code comment — I fed the estimator two different 'ego states,' one correct (matching the track's own timestamp) and one from a later, faster-moving ego, and confirmed they give measurably different answers, so the test would actually fail if a future change accidentally used the wrong one."
- "I deliberately built the more cautious 'conservative TTC' calculation now, ahead of when it's actually needed, but explicitly did NOT wire it into the real action decision — that separation between 'available for later' and 'load-bearing today' is a specific, documented decision (DEC-10), not an oversight."

## Common misunderstandings
- **"NOT_ELIGIBLE and 'infinite TTC' mean the same thing operationally."** They're stored the same way (`ttc_s = infinity`) but mean opposite things: `NOT_ELIGIBLE` means "no usable answer exists yet," while a genuinely computed infinite TTC (from Part A's deadbands) means "an answer exists, and it says there's no danger." Conflating them would be exactly the mistake step07 §B3 forbids.
- **"The critical-object selection should just pick whichever track is closest."** It picks the smallest TIME, not the smallest DISTANCE — a nearby but slow-closing (or even receding) object can have a much larger, safer TTC than a farther but fast-closing one.
- **"Since the conservative TTC bound exists in the code, it must already be influencing behavior."** It's computed and available, but explicitly not connected to the action decision yet — a deliberate, recorded choice (DEC-10), not a half-finished feature.
